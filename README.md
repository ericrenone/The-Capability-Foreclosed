# The Capability Foreclosed: Tacit Knowledge, Motivational Displacement, and the Critical Theory of Attentional Misallocation

> "We can know more than we can tell. This is the tacit dimension."
> — Michael Polanyi, *The Tacit Dimension*, 1966

> "Entry-level automation raises short-run productivity but weakens the skills of future generations, slowing long-run growth — sometimes enough to reduce welfare."
> — Enrique Ide, arXiv:2507.16078 (R&R, *American Economic Review*), 2026

> "By telling workers that their inadequate education is to blame for their troubles, meritocrats moralize success and failure and unwittingly promote credentialism — an insidious prejudice."
> — Michael Sandel, *The Tyranny of Merit*, 2020

> "The pathologies of late capitalism are caused by the colonization of the lifeworld by the system."
> — Jürgen Habermas, *The Theory of Communicative Action*, 1981

---

## Abstract

Three prior companions to the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework — a concept whose architecture draws on the limits-of-arbitrage tradition in financial economics (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997) and on Simon's (1971) foundational analysis of attention as a scarce resource in information-rich environments — addressed the economic, psychological, and sociological dimensions of cognitive capital mispricing under machine capability expansion. This fourth companion takes up the questions none of them addressed: why machine-resistant capabilities are machine-resistant, at the deepest epistemological level; what machine capability expansion does to the intergenerational chain through which those capabilities are transmitted; what is morally wrong with meritocratic prestige systems beyond their allocative inefficiency; why the prestige competition structure systematically destroys the motivational conditions that machine-resistant capabilities require; and what philosophical framework allows us to name the stakes correctly — not as wasted productivity but as foreclosed human capability. The framework required to answer these questions is not economics, psychology, or sociology. It is philosophy: philosophy of knowledge, critical theory, and normative political philosophy. These four traditions converge on a single claim that the economic framework implies but cannot articulate: what is being destroyed by the mispricing is not just return on investment. It is the substance of what it means to know.

## I. Why Machine-Resistant Capabilities Are Machine-Resistant: The Philosophy of Tacit Knowledge

The *Attentional Arbitrage* framework identifies machine-resistant cognitive capabilities as the long position in the attentional arbitrage: tasks requiring open-world problem framing, metacognitive calibration under genuine uncertainty, and adaptive judgment in novel contexts. It identifies these capabilities empirically, through the jagged frontier — the task categories on which frontier AI systems continue to fail (Kapoor et al., 2026; Stanford HAI, 2026). The persistence of that frontier, recently documented through benchmark saturation on closed-domain tasks alongside continued failure on genuine open-world challenges (Phan et al., 2026; Kapoor et al., 2026), does not explain *why* those categories are structurally resistant to machine substitution. The explanation is philosophical, and it is Polanyi's.

Michael Polanyi's *The Tacit Dimension* (1966) establishes the foundational epistemological claim: we can know more than we can tell. All skilled performance involves a dimension of tacit knowing — a subsidiary awareness of particular details in the service of a focal achievement — that cannot be fully articulated without destroying the skill itself. The expert pianist attending to the meaning of the music cannot simultaneously attend to the precise position of each finger; making the subsidiary focal destroys the focal performance. The skilled physician recognizing a diagnosis cannot fully articulate the pattern of cues that generated the recognition without losing the gestalt. Tacit knowing is constitutively resistant to explicit codification, not merely practically difficult to articulate.

This is not a temporary limitation. Harry Collins and Robert Evans's program of Studies in Expertise and Experience, crystallized in Collins and Evans (2007) and Collins's (2010) taxonomy of tacit knowledge, establishes why Polanyi's claim has direct bearing on machine capability limits. Collins (2010) distinguishes three forms of tacit knowledge: relational tacit knowledge, which is kept implicit by social convention and could in principle be made explicit; somatic tacit knowledge, embedded in bodily skill and constitutively bound to human physical engagement; and collective tacit knowledge, embedded in social practices that no individual fully possesses or can fully articulate, existing only in the distributed enactment of the community. Machine systems can absorb relational tacit knowledge through large-scale data processing. They cannot, in principle, acquire somatic or collective tacit knowledge — not because of computational limitations but because of the ontological structure of those knowledge forms. They require a body and a social life to exist. Recent organizational research has deepened this account: Hadjimichael, Ribeiro, and Tsoukas (2024) demonstrate, drawing on Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of embodiment, that somatic tacit knowledge is not merely stored in the body but is constituted through the lived, situated engagement of a body in a world — a structure that computational systems cannot replicate in principle, not merely in practice.

Ide's (2026) formalization of the intergenerational transmission of tacit knowledge provides the precise economic consequence. Tacit knowledge is acquired by novices through sustained interaction with experts — through apprenticeship-like arrangements in which repeated observation of practical successes and failures, in the company of someone who embeds those outcomes in a normative frame, gradually builds the subsidiary awareness structure that constitutes expertise. AI-driven entry-level automation disrupts this transmission precisely because it substitutes machine performance for the entry-level execution that novices need to observe and participate in. Senior workers can accomplish more tasks independently. They therefore need fewer novices. The implicit contract through which novices exchange labor for training breaks down. Ide's (2026) equilibrium result is stark: socially excessive automation of entry-level tasks raises short-run productivity while undermining the intergenerational transmission of the tacit skills the economy needs, with back-of-the-envelope estimates suggesting AI-driven entry-level automation could reduce the long-run annual U.S. growth rate by 0.05 to 0.35 percentage points.

The philosophical implication for the attentional arbitrage framework: the long position — machine-resistant cognitive capability — is machine-resistant precisely because it is tacitly grounded. The capabilities at the jagged frontier are not simply difficult for machines; they are constitutively different in kind from what machine systems can hold. The epistemological claim is prior to the economic one. The question "which cognitive capabilities retain premium value under machine capability expansion?" has a deeper answer than "the ones that haven't been saturated yet." The answer is: the ones constituted by forms of knowing that cannot, in principle, be codified and therefore cannot, in principle, be absorbed into any system that processes codified information — however much of it.

## II. The Intergenerational Trap: What the Misallocation Destroys Beyond the Present Generation

Every prior companion to the framework analyzed cognitive capital misallocation as a contemporaneous problem: individual agents investing in low-return domains when higher-return alternatives exist. Ide's (2026) model reveals that the misallocation is also intergenerational, and the intergenerational dimension changes the moral and economic stakes fundamentally.

The transmission of tacit knowledge requires a structured temporal relationship between novice and expert. The novice must be present as the expert works — not observing outputs but participating in the cognitive and practical process through which expert judgment is formed and exercised. This is not mentorship as a nice-to-have organizational feature. It is the mechanism through which collective tacit knowledge — the distributed social knowledge that constitutes expert practice in any domain — is reproduced across generations. Without this transmission, expertise does not depreciate slowly; it fails to form.

Ide (2026) demonstrates that AI-driven entry-level automation generates an intergenerational trade-off that market mechanisms cannot correct. The competitive equilibrium features socially excessive automation of early-career tasks because the individual firm captures the short-run productivity gain while the social cost — the erosion of future expertise supply — is diffuse, delayed, and falls on future generations who are not party to the current contracting. The knowledge-transfer contract between expert and novice is incomplete because tacit knowledge is embodied and non-verifiable: neither party can write a contract whose fulfilment a court could monitor. The result is a negative externality whose magnitude is invisible to the price system at the moment it is being generated. Shen and Tamkin (2026), analyzing how AI tools affect skill formation in knowledge workers, reach a structurally similar conclusion through a different empirical lens: AI assistance accelerates task completion while attenuating the deliberate practice through which skills deepen, with negative effects concentrated in early-career workers whose expertise is most actively in formation. Peterson (2025) puts the institutional dimension most sharply, arguing that educational systems optimizing for near-term AI-measurable performance are systematically training students for the automated jobs of the present rather than the judgment-requiring roles of the future — a training-for-obsolescence dynamic that Ide's (2026) model provides the micro-foundations to explain.

Applied to the cognitive domains the attentional arbitrage framework identifies as prestigious but machine-saturated — spelling, chess, bounded computation — the intergenerational analysis reveals a second-order harm beyond the contemporaneous misallocation. The children investing thousands of hours in these domains are not merely forgoing contemporaneous return. They are failing to enter the apprenticeship structures through which the tacit knowledge of machine-resistant practice is transmitted. The machine-saturated domain does not merely misprice; it terminates the intergenerational chain of tacit transmission for the domain's practitioners. When that chain terminates, the expertise it was transmitting does not simply become scarcer and more valuable. It becomes unavailable — because it was constituted by collective tacit knowledge that could not survive the dissolution of the community of practice.

## III. The Meritocracy Problem: Sandel and the Political Philosophy of Prestige

The economic framework diagnoses attentional mispricing as an allocative problem. The psychological framework diagnoses it as an identity problem. The sociological framework diagnoses it as a reproductive problem. Each diagnosis is correct within its disciplinary frame. What none of them names is the moral problem — the harm that the prestige system inflicts not through misallocation, not through identity foreclosure, not through social reproduction, but through the moralization of cognitive achievement that meritocratic systems perform.

Michael Sandel's *The Tyranny of Merit* (2020) identifies this moral problem with precision. Meritocratic systems do not merely sort people into winners and losers. They tell the winners that they deserve to win — that their success is a reflection of their superior virtue — and they tell the losers that they deserve to lose — that their failure is a product of their own inadequacy. This moralization of outcomes generates what Sandel (2020) calls meritocratic hubris among the winners and a systematic humiliation of those left behind, a combination that erodes the sense of shared social life and common dignity that democratic politics requires.

Applied to the prestige competitions the framework diagnoses as mispriced, Sandel's (2020) analysis reveals that the harm is not merely economic but civic. The child who invests thousands of hours in a spelling competition and achieves national recognition does not merely receive a misdirected price signal. They receive a moral affirmation — an institutional certification that their effort and talent are of the kind that society values — that they will internalize as a constitutive judgment about their worth. When machine capability eliminates the productive relationship that once justified that certification, the moral affirmation does not disappear. It persists as an orphaned warrant for self-esteem, attached to an activity whose social value has collapsed but whose prestige infrastructure — the competitions, the sponsorships, the national coverage — continues to transmit the moral signal.

The political philosophical dimension compounds the economic one in a specific way that neither prior README captured. Sandel (2020) argues that credentialism — the meritocratic insistence that educational credentials are the primary route to a respectable life — constitutes the last socially acceptable form of status-based contempt. Disdain for the poorly credentialed, he observes, is now expressed openly in contexts where racial or gender contempt would not be tolerated. AI capability expansion does not merely devalue certain credentials in the labor market. It expands the scope of contempt that the credential system licenses: as machine capability absorbs the cognitive tasks that prestigious credentials certified, the social recognition those credentials carried migrates to a narrowing set of demonstrations, and those outside that set face an intensified version of the moral judgment that credentialism has always rendered against those who cannot satisfy it.

This is not a marginal observation about hurt feelings. It is a claim about the erosion of democratic dignity at precisely the moment when the AI transition requires the broadest possible social trust and civic cooperation for its costs to be distributed justly and its benefits to flow to those most exposed to its disruption.

## IV. Motivational Displacement: Self-Determination Theory and the Destruction of Intrinsic Drive

The psychological companion to this framework analyzed cognitive substrate destruction — the mechanism through which AI delegation at the formation stage destroys the capability being formed. What it did not analyze is the motivational destruction that precedes the cognitive: the mechanism through which prestige competition structures displace the intrinsic motivation that machine-resistant capability formation requires.

Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (SDT), developed across decades of empirical research (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2017), establishes three basic psychological needs whose satisfaction is necessary for autonomous, intrinsically motivated engagement: autonomy, the experience of initiative and ownership over one's actions; competence, the sense of genuine mastery and growth; and relatedness, a sense of social connection in the activity. When these needs are satisfied, intrinsic motivation flourishes and produces the kind of sustained, deep engagement that builds durable capability. When external controls — rewards, rankings, surveillance, competitive evaluation — become the primary motivational architecture, intrinsic motivation is displaced, replaced by externally regulated behavior whose cognitive engagement is shallower and whose capability-building consequence is weaker (Deci & Ryan, 2000). The most recent SDT synthesis confirms that this crowding-out dynamic is robust across developmental stages and domain types, with particularly strong effects in high-stakes competitive evaluation contexts (Deci & Ryan, 2020).

Prestige competitions are, by design, architectures of external control. They provide ranked outcomes, public evaluation, scholarship rewards, and social recognition contingent on performance — precisely the motivational structures that SDT predicts will undermine autonomous engagement. The student competing in a national spelling competition is not primarily motivated by intrinsic interest in orthography; they are motivated by the external rewards the competition makes salient. Under SDT, this extrinsic motivational structure produces effortful compliance but not the autonomous engagement through which genuine capability is built and generalized.

The SDT analysis creates a motivational paradox that the prior framework companions did not identify. Machine-resistant capabilities — the long position in the attentional arbitrage — require precisely the motivational architecture that prestige competitions destroy: sustained autonomous engagement with open problems, intrinsic curiosity about uncertainty, willingness to persist in difficulty without external validation. These motivational features are not merely helpful for developing machine-resistant capability; they are constitutive of it. The capacity to frame novel problems, to sustain judgment under genuine ambiguity, to calibrate confidence in the absence of authoritative feedback — these are practices that develop only under conditions of autonomous engagement (Ryan & Deci, 2017), not under conditions of external rank optimization.

The prestige competition therefore harms twice. It directs cognitive investment toward machine-saturated domains, as the economic framework establishes. And it shapes the motivational structure of that investment in ways that specifically undermine the conditions under which the investment could have been redirected productively. The agent who leaves a prestige competition environment does not arrive at open-world problem spaces with motivational resources intact. They arrive having been trained by years of external evaluation to seek clear correct answers, ranked feedback, and authoritative validation — the motivational profile most incompatible with genuine engagement in the domain they need to enter.

## V. The Colonization of the Educational Lifeworld: Habermas and the Critical Theory Diagnosis

The deepest theoretical register in which the attentional mispricing can be placed is Habermas's (1984, 1987) distinction between the lifeworld and the system, and his diagnosis of colonization: the process by which the steering media of the system — money and power — invade and displace the communicative practices through which the lifeworld is constituted and reproduced.

The lifeworld, in Habermas's (1984) framework, is the domain of shared meanings, values, and social practices in which human development, education, cultural formation, and democratic deliberation take place. These practices are constituted by communicative rationality — the orientation toward mutual understanding, shared meaning-making, and genuine epistemic engagement that distinguishes human development from mere instrumental optimization. The system, by contrast, operates through money and power as steering media, coordinating action without requiring communicative engagement, substituting price signals and authority relations for genuine understanding (Habermas, 1987).

The prestige competition structure that the attentional arbitrage framework diagnoses as mispriced is, in Habermasian terms, a site of systemic colonization of educational lifeworld practices. Education, as a lifeworld institution, is constituted by communicative practices: the genuine encounter between a developing mind and a domain of knowledge, mediated by a teacher who participates as a person in that encounter. Prestige competitions colonize this practice by substituting the steering media of the system — national rankings, scholarship dollars, professional recognition — for the communicative orientation that genuine education requires (Habermas, 1987). The child in the spelling competition is not engaging communicatively with the domain of language; they are optimizing for a metric that the system has made available as a proxy for educational achievement.

AI-optimized prestige systems intensify this colonization by adding a third layer of systemic mediation: the algorithmic sorting and ranking of cognitive performances through machine assessment. The Stanford HAI (2026) Index documents the accelerating expansion of AI evaluation systems into educational settings, precisely as frontier AI saturates the narrow task domains those settings have historically valued. Where educational practice was first colonized by the money and power of credential competition, it is now being further colonized by the computational steering media of algorithmic classification. Habermas's diagnosis predicts precisely the pathologies that the empirical literature documents: the displacement of intrinsic motivation by external optimization (Deci & Ryan, 2000), the attrition of communicative engagement in favor of metric performance (Peterson, 2025), and the progressive erosion of the conditions under which genuine human development — the development of the capacities for autonomous judgment that Habermas (1984) identifies as constitutive of communicative competence — is possible.

The critical theory diagnosis is not pessimistic about AI itself, any more than Habermas was pessimistic about markets and bureaucracies per se. The system is necessary; the question is whether it remains accountable to and bounded by the communicative rationality of the lifeworld it is meant to serve. The attentional arbitrage framework, read through Habermas, is a call not merely for individual reallocation but for the re-establishment of communicative rationality as the governing criterion of educational practice — the insistence that what we are developing in cognitive education is not optimizable performance on legible metrics but the substantive capacity for autonomous judgment that no metric can adequately capture.

## VI. What Is Actually at Stake: Sen's Capability Approach as the Correct Normative Frame

All four framework companions — economic, psychological, sociological, and now philosophical — share a limitation in their normative framing. They diagnose the mispricing as a misallocation of cognitive capital, a misdirection of investment, a loss of return. These framings are correct but insufficient. They measure what is lost in the currency of the system — productivity, return, status — rather than in the currency of human life.

Amartya Sen's (1999) capability approach provides the correct normative frame. Sen argues that the appropriate unit of evaluation for social arrangements is not welfare, resources, or opportunity alone but capabilities — the real freedoms people have to live the lives they have reason to value. A capability is not a resource and not an achievement; it is the substantive opportunity to achieve a functioning — the combination of being and doing that constitutes human flourishing in a given domain. The capability approach is prior to economic evaluation rather than reducible to it: a society that maximizes aggregate productivity while foreclosing the development of the capabilities that constitute a fully human cognitive life has failed on Sen's (1999) account even if it succeeds on standard economic measures.

Applied to the attentional mispricing, Sen's framework reveals that what the misallocation destroys is not return on investment. It is capability — the real opportunity to develop and exercise the forms of knowing and judgment that constitute a fully human cognitive life. The child investing thousands of hours in spelling bee preparation is not merely receiving a bad investment signal. They are being deprived, by a structurally corrupt prestige system, of the substantive opportunity to develop the capabilities whose exercise would constitute genuine human cognitive flourishing: the capacity for creative problem framing, for genuine epistemic engagement with uncertainty, for the exercise of judgment in the face of novel challenges.

Martha Nussbaum's (2011) extension of Sen's framework identifies a canonical set of central human capabilities that a just social arrangement must protect and promote. Her list includes practical reason — the capacity to form a conception of the good and engage in critical reflection about the planning of one's own life — and play — the capacity to enjoy recreational activities. Both are directly implicated in the attentional mispricing. The development of practical reason requires exactly the open-world cognitive engagement that prestige competitions foreclose. The conversion of play — the intrinsic engagement with challenges for their own interest — into competitive performance optimization is precisely the motivational displacement that Deci and Ryan (2000) and Ryan and Deci (2017) predict will corrode autonomous development. Nussbaum's (2011) framework demands that we evaluate the prestige competition system by what it does to these capabilities — not by what it does to test scores, scholarship disbursements, or labor market returns.

The normative implication of the capability frame is the sharpest statement the framework can make: the attentional mispricing is not a market failure. It is a capability deprivation — a systematic social arrangement that forecloses the real freedom of the people most subject to it to develop the capabilities that constitute a flourishing cognitive life in the age of machine intelligence. Correcting it is not an optimization problem. It is a justice problem.
